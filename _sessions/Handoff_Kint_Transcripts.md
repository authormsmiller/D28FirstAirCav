## Interview Structure — Segment Summaries

### Segment 1: Pre-Service Background and Draft
**Approximate runtime:** [00:00:00 – 00:12:04]

**Core content:**
- Born February 1945, Iowa City, IA; grew up in Cedar Rapids, then Manchester, IA (Delaware County seat)
- Graduated Manchester High School 1963; enrolled State College of Iowa (now UNI), graduated 1967
- Returned to Manchester area to teach junior high science and math — a deliberate career choice; taught three years
- Draft lottery number: **182**; among the last (or near-last) of six men from Delaware County called that year
- Was approximately **six months from aging out** of draft eligibility
- Did not seek deferment
- Inducted at Des Moines, Iowa

**Tone and texture:** Methodical, calm. Kint frames his draft story not with resentment but with an almost actuarial precision — he was 25, single, no children, lottery hit 182. He was due.

**Site development potential:**
- The "six months from aging out" detail is a compelling near-miss frame for his pre-service section
- The deliberate teaching career, then interruption by draft: strong biographical arc opener

### Segment 2: Basic Training, AIT, and the MOS Logic Battle
**Approximate runtime:** [00:12:04 – 00:27:05]

**Core content:**

*Fort Leonard Wood basic training (arrived August 1970):*
- Older than most trainees and older than the lieutenants drilling him
- Memorized M16 dimensions and specs so thoroughly drill sergeants who tried to grill him in formation eventually moved on
- Offered OCS based on test scores — declined (wanted to go back to teaching)
- Assigned 11B Infantry at end of basic; did not anticipate this

*The MOS argument (first attempt):*
- Went to an officer and argued, with "characteristic logic," that his science background suited him for a lab tech role
- "The argument was coherent. It made no difference."

*Fort Polk AIT (no leave between):*
- Barracks in D-43
- Made the MOS argument a **second time to a second officer** — same result
- Offered OCS again; declined again
- Took infantry training seriously because the instructors "had already been to Vietnam and knew what they were talking about"
- Understood clearly: the only place the Army was currently sending infantry was Vietnam
- Achieved **expert marksman qualification** on multiple weapons
- "He trusted the system to give him the tools he would need."

*The Belly-First Lesson:*
- Fort Polk instructors taught trainees to hit the ground belly-first, not on their backs
- Training method: flour bags thrown at trainees to condition the response
- This lesson becomes directly relevant in his first firefight in Vietnam (see Segment 3)

*Transit to Vietnam:*
- Fort Lewis, Washington → Alaska → Japan
- Fully contained flight throughout — no duty-free, no civilian contact
- Arriving Japan: noted anti-aircraft gun structures from WWII still standing at runway ends

**Tone and texture:** Kint's MOS argument story is told with dry humor and self-awareness — he knew the argument was good; he also knew it wasn't going to work. The repetition (twice to two different officers) makes it funnier and more pointed.

**Site development potential:**
- The MOS argument × 2: compact, self-contained, publishable as a short narrative
- The Fort Polk–to–first-firefight connection (flour bag lesson → green tracers): strong structural payoff requiring both segments to land; works best as part of a longer piece

### Segment 3: Arrival, Walking Point, and First Firefight
**Approximate runtime:** [00:27:05 – 00:36:19] (overlap with Segment 2 end)

**Core content:**

*Arrival (December 10, 1970):*
- Bob Hope Christmas show the following week; as a "cherry" he pulled duty while veterans attended
- One-week in-country tactical update on current booby-trap types and VC methodology

*Assignment to D/1-8 Cav:*
- Transported to a forward triangular firebase in MR3
- CO was from Eldridge, Iowa — discovered when Kint went to argue his first in-country assignment
- Platoon lieutenant was a Dartmouth graduate who called their column the **"OD Circus"**

*Walking point (The Orphan Logic):*
- In his first week, squad leader assigned him to walk point
- Kint went to the Company Commander to contest it
- CO's reasoning, in full: Army policy — avoid creating orphans and widows. Married? No. Children? He counted his former students; the CO did not count them. Someone had DEROSed, slot needed filling, Kint fit the profile.
- Three platoons rotated the lead daily — Kint walked point roughly **one day in three**

*The VC Black Pajama Factory and First Firefight:*
- Platoon swept through a hidden VC uniform factory: treadle sewing machines, bolts of black cloth, garments half-cut and half-sewn, abandoned moments before the platoon arrived
- VC rear guard opened fire to cover the withdrawal
- **Kint went down on his back** — exactly the mistake he had feared since the flour-bag exercise at Fort Polk
- "Looking straight up at the sky, he watched green enemy tracer rounds tracking down toward his face, converging, getting closer. They stopped. He was certain he was dead."
- Then they started again; he rolled over and achieved fire superiority
- Described as his first "I just missed it" moment. "There would be others."

**Tone and texture:** The green tracer story is one of the most cinematically vivid moments in the interview. The setup (flour bag lesson at Fort Polk) and the payoff (landing on his back in a real firefight) is a complete narrative structure delivered across two separate interviews. Kint tells it without drama — "I was certain I was dead" — which makes it more powerful.

**Site development potential:**
- **Highest priority.** The flour-bag-to-tracer arc is a standalone story with a beginning, middle, end, and payoff. Strong candidate for a dedicated "Stories" or "Portraits" page.
- The "Orphan Logic" (CO's point assignment reasoning) is also complete and publishable; shorter.
- The OD Circus nickname: useful for future platoon/company page context.

### Segment 4: Life in the Bush — Sensory and Operational Detail
**Approximate runtime:** [00:36:19 – 00:57:42]

**Core content:**

*The Post-Cambodia Shift:*
- Described his tour as "the easy year" — 1970 Cambodian Incursion had swept large NVA supply caches out of the jungle; by 1971 contacts were small and localized rather than massed regimental engagements
- What he experienced: not "days of boredom, seconds of terror" — instead a "continuous, unrelenting knot in the stomach." Every hour of every patrol: scanning for tripwires, watching for movement, looking for eyes.

*Weapons and gear philosophy:*
- Issued M16 with M79 40mm grenade launcher underslung ("over-under")
- Dedicated vest with low pouches for 79 rounds: HE, buckshot, marking, illumination
- Pack stripped to minimum — needed to drop to his stomach without being pinned
- No helmet when he could manage it: fashioned a headband from underwear waistband and an OD handkerchief as camo for his bald head
- A subsequent CO ordered him to wear the helmet; he complied and "considered it largely irrelevant — if a round was going to find him, it would go through the helmet anyway"

*The "Baby Rattle" ammo boxes:*
- Soldiers taped ammo magazines to prevent the "baby rattle" noise during movement
- Operational noise discipline detail

*Sleeping arrangements:*
- In the field: on the ground on a poncho, boots on, M16 within reach
- At the firebase: inside drainage culvert sections with sandbags overhead — hot, humid, and "situated directly on the rat highway between the local rat population and the mess hall"
- "He worried about dying of a rat bite."

*Wild elephants:*
- Elephant herds came through the bamboo on three or four nights — feeding during cool hours, easily spooked, capable of trampling anything in their path
- **"He did not find this amusing."**
- Monkeys played in the trees on other nights; he hoped nobody would open fire on them

*Food and supplies:*
- C-rations and MREs; heated freeze-dried meals with water and heat tabs
- Everyone carried Heinz 57, A1 sauce, or hot sauce
- Pound cake from C-rations was worth keeping; most of the rest was not
- PX catalog "as thick as a Sears catalog" circulated through the unit
- Ordered a camera at some point; "documented very little with it"
- Carried family photos and writing materials in his pack

*Radio discipline:*
- Handset passed around the perimeter through the night
- Guard acknowledged by breaking squelch twice — pressing the button, no words, just the sound
- "No voices, no position given away."

*The 8th grade letters:*
- A colleague — an older female English teacher — had been forwarding 8th grade English class assignments to him in the field
- Formal exercises: complete sentences, proper punctuation, structured paragraphs
- Other soldiers in the unit wanted to read them
- **"Reading them gave Kint the only sensation that approaching the whole thing made sense — that surviving it might be worth something. He had no wife or children to think about. The letters were his one psychological anchor."**

*The Montagnard bracelet:*
- Still wears it; made by hill people of MR3 from stripped electrical cord insulation
- They wanted American goods; Army issued military payment certificates instead of dollars to prevent a black market
- "He has no memory of making it to any villages."

*Unit redesignation:*
- D/1-8 Cav → D/2-8 Cav as part of the drawdown
- Occurred while unit was deep in the bush, entirely cut off from rear communications
- "Kint and his squad had no idea the redesignation had occurred. They found out later."

*Chieu Hoi operations:*
- Setting up night locations along known trails for safe passage of Vietnamese civilians moving away from VC-controlled areas
- "The VC were not gentle with villagers who tried to leave. His unit didn't shoot at people moving in the dark toward the safe zones. He felt good about that."

**Tone and texture:** Segment 4 is the richest for sensory and environmental detail. Kint's register shifts here — less analytical, more observational. The rat highway, the elephants, the letters: these are the moments that reveal the emotional texture of the tour.

**Site development potential:**
- **The 8th grade letters** — Standalone story. Complete with backstory, emotional stakes, and resolution (the colleagues, the anchor, surviving meaning something). Highly publishable.
- **Wild elephants and rats** — Contrast piece: the absurdity of worrying about rats after surviving firefights, and the genuine fear of a 5-ton animal in the dark. Short, punchy.
- **The Montagnard bracelet** — Good closing detail for a longer piece; the last line ("no memory of making it to any villages") is quietly devastating.
- **The redesignation** — Illustrates the disconnect between field reality and administrative reality; useful in any contextual piece about the drawdown.
- **Chieu Hoi operations** — Important for any discussion of the moral complexity of the tour; Kint volunteers this detail and emphasizes that it felt right.

### Segment 5: The Rear, Vũng Tàu, Coming Home, and the School Board
**Approximate runtime:** [00:57:42 – end]

**Core content:**

*Reassignment to Biên Hòa as company clerk typist (~October 1971):*
- After approximately ten months in the bush, pulled back to Biên Hòa Air Base
- "It was the first moment he allowed himself to believe he might make it home."
- Hot showers. A mess hall. Movies projected onto the side of a building.
- Stayed within the 1st Cavalry compound; did not go into town
- Typed his own DEROS paperwork

*Vũng Tàu (three trips total):*
- First two: in-country R&R passes while still in the bush, granted after contacts
- Helicopter to a strip, then loaded onto a C-130 that climbed out at an angle "that made him grip the webbing and look straight down out the back of the aircraft"
- Three days each time
- Vũng Tàu: the old French resort town on the coast; hotels, restaurants, beaches; military R&R center with tents, cots, mess hall, surfboards
- "He knew there were no snipers and no land mines. He described it as a quantum leap from his day-to-day experience."
- Has photographs from Vũng Tàu
- Third trip: logistics run as clerk; he and the First Sergeant drove a jeep towing a supply trailer loaded with clean shirts and supplies from Biên Hòa to the R&R center; saw a rear-echelon soldier cruising streets on a Honda 750 with a custom fuel tank — "He did not consider this fair."

*Departure (December 8, 1971):*
- Standard Army Christmas-season compression policy: last days trimmed off calendar
- Had missed the 1970 Bob Hope show as a cherry; was gone before the 1971 show arrived — **bookend structure**
- Processed out through Oakland in the middle of the night
- One free steak at 2 a.m.
- Told to wear civilian clothes for transit home; only clothes available were PX: plaid shirts, form-fitting slacks, "nothing resembling the bell-bottom era outside the gate"
- "He looked, he acknowledged, extraordinarily dorky." Buzzcut. Olive-drab duffel bag.
- Sat at the airport before it opened, first in line. "People looked. Nobody said anything."
- Flew to Cedar Rapids; parents, aunt, uncle met him at the airport in the middle of the night
- "Nobody asked what he had done. They were glad he was home."

*Decompression (Manchester, Christmas 1972):*
- Bought a motorcycle
- Friends took him riding through woodlots and farms; they went to work during the day; he kept riding
- "He described it as decompression. He doesn't know if it would have worked for everyone, but it worked for him."

*The School Board Battle:*
- Public school districts legally exempt from federal statutes requiring reinstatement at earned pay level
- His principal had not known this; held his position year by year with temporary hires
- Contract listed him as a fourth-year teacher — under federal law applying to every other employer, he should have been credited for years he would have accumulated
- Put on the suit tailored in **Hong Kong during R&R**, went to the school board, and made his case
- Two board members took exception
- His argument: "If I had spent my service behind a desk stateside, I would not be standing here — I would have been glad to have my job back at any pay grade. I have been shot at. I have shot at people. This is not how it works anywhere else."
- Board members: "We don't, in fact, have to follow the same rules as anywhere else."
- Superintendent retrieved personnel file of a vocal music teacher who had started in 1967 alongside Kint, been drafted in 1968, returned in 1970 — and had, through clerical good faith or mistake, been placed at his correct step upon return
- Superintendent slid the folder in front of the most vocal board member
- Board member "opened it, went visibly pale, slammed it shut, and said nothing further"
- Superintendent: "It appears the district has an established precedent."
- **Kint won.** Returned at his earned pay grade.
- "He managed the flashbacks that came — a student's challenging tone, a noise, something in the air. He kept a lid on it and was later surprised that he had managed to."
- The board member's two sons eventually came through his classroom. He treated them well.
- Final line: "He never again landed on his back."

**Tone and texture:** The school board story is Kint at his most structured and deliberate — the same methodical logic he applied to the MOS argument, this time with stakes and a villain. The Hong Kong suit detail is perfect. The final line of the interview ("He never again landed on his back") closes the loop on the flour-bag story from Segment 2 — though it's used here metaphorically to mean he never again went down without a fighting chance.

**Site development potential:**
- **The school board battle** — Full standalone narrative. Beginning (the draft, teaching career interrupted), middle (the argument, the opposition), end (superintendent's gambit, Kint wins), coda (board member's sons). Possibly the best complete story in the archive.
- **The Bob Hope bookends** — Compact, structurally elegant. Works as an opener or closer for any biography piece. Missed the 1970 show as a cherry. Gone before the 1971 show. The Army's compression policy as accidental symmetry.
- **Oakland at 2am** — Short, vivid, self-deprecating. The plaid PX shirts. The duffel bag. "Extraordinarily dorky." First in line before the airport opened.
- **"Nobody asked what he had done"** — The homecoming in one sentence.
- **The motorcycle** — Four sentences that say everything about decompression and the difficulty of returning.
