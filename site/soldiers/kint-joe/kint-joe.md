bio: >
  Joe Kint was born in Iowa City in February 1945. He grew up in Cedar Rapids before his family
  moved to Manchester, Iowa (Delaware County), where he graduated from Manchester High School in
  1963. He attended State College of Iowa (now University of Northern Iowa), graduating in 1967,
  and returned to the Manchester area where he taught junior high science and mathematics for three
  years. In the summer of 1970, he was drafted — lottery number 182, among the last (or
  near-last) of six men from Delaware County called that year. He was approximately six months
  from aging out of draft eligibility. He completed basic training at Fort Leonard Wood, Missouri,
  and Infantry AIT at Fort Polk, Louisiana — twice attempting, through sheer teacher's logic, to
  argue his way into a laboratory technician assignment. He was assigned 11B Infantry both times.
  He earned expert marksman qualification on multiple weapons during training. He transited to
  Vietnam via Fort Lewis, Washington, flying through Alaska and Japan before landing in-country,
  where he completed a one-week tactical update on current booby-trap and enemy methodology before
  deploying to a forward firebase to join his company. He arrived in-country assigned to
  Delta Company, 1st Battalion, 8th Cavalry, and left as Delta Company, 2nd Battalion, 8th
  Cavalry — the redesignation occurring as part of the 1971 drawdown consolidation while his
  unit was deep in the bush, so cut off from rear communications that he and his squad did not
  know their battalion designation had changed until long after the fact. In his first week
  in-country, Kint was assigned to walk point. He went to the Company Commander — from Eldridge,
  Iowa — to argue against it, as he had argued twice against his infantry MOS. The CO offered
  the same institutional logic: Army policy was to avoid creating orphans or widows, and Kint
  was unmarried with no children. He walked point. The platoon lieutenant, a Dartmouth graduate,
  called their tactical column the "OD Circus." Early in his tour, his platoon stumbled onto an
  active Viet Cong uniform factory concealed in the brush — treadle sewing machines, bolts of
  black cloth, garments half-finished — abandoned moments before their arrival. A VC rear guard
  opened fire to cover the withdrawal. In the chaos, Kint made exactly the training error he had
  feared since the flour-bag exercise at Fort Polk: he went down on his back. Looking straight
  up into the jungle canopy, he watched green enemy tracer rounds track closer and closer down
  his line of sight. They stopped. He was convinced he was already dead — then they started
  again, and he rolled over. It was his first "I just missed it" moment. There would be others.
  His unit operated in III Corps along the Cambodian border, with Biên Hòa as their rear base.
  The 1970 Cambodian Incursion had stripped the major NVA supply caches from the area; by 1971,
  Kint's unit faced smaller, sharper, localized contacts rather than the massed engagements of
  the late 1960s. He described the psychological reality not as boredom punctuated by terror but
  as a continuous, unrelenting knot in the stomach — scanning for tripwires, eyes in the brush,
  things hanging in bamboo — every hour of every patrol. He was later issued an M16 with an M79
  grenade launcher underslung, the "over-under," and deliberately kept his pack stripped to the
  minimum so nothing would slow a stomach-first drop. He slept on the ground on a poncho, boots
  on and M16 at hand; at the firebase, in drainage culvert sections with sandbags overhead. The
  radio handset went around the perimeter through the night — guard was signaled by breaking
  squelch twice rather than speaking. Wild elephant herds crashed through the bamboo on three or
  four nights. He received packets of 8th grade English assignments forwarded from a colleague
  in Iowa a couple of times during his tour — structured letters with complete sentences and
  punctuation — the only thing that gave him a sense that surviving was worth it. He still wears
  a Montagnard bracelet, made by hill people of III Corps from stripped electrical wire.
  After approximately ten months in the bush, he was pulled back to Biên Hòa Air Base and
  reassigned as company clerk typist — the moment he first allowed himself to believe he
  might make it home. He made three trips to Vũng Tàu during his tour: two in-country R&R
  passes while still in the bush (a steep C-130 climb out, three days at the beach resort
  the French had built), and a final logistics run as clerk, driving a jeep with a supply
  trailer of clean uniforms down the road from Biên Hòa. He departed Vietnam approximately
  December 8, 1971 — two days short of a full twelve months. Processed through Oakland,
  California, in the dead of night; ate the free steak at 2 a.m.; wore PX civilian clothes
  (a plaid shirt and form-fitting slacks) per military orders to blend in, though his buzzcut
  and olive-drab duffel gave him away. He flew home to Cedar Rapids, Iowa, where his parents
  and extended family met him at the airport in the middle of the night. He spent the following
  month in Manchester at Christmas, decompressing on motorcycles with friends in the woodlots.
  He could not return to teaching until the following August — public schools are exempt from
  the federal law requiring employers to reinstate veterans at their earned pay level. His
  principal had held his job, unaware the district had no legal obligation to do so. When Kint
  returned and found his contract listed him as a fourth-year teacher rather than at the seniority
  level he would have reached, he went to the school board wearing a suit he had tailored in
  Hong Kong on R&R. Two board members opposed his claim. The superintendent resolved the dispute
  by producing the personnel file of a vocal music teacher who had been drafted in 1968 and
  returned in 1970 — through a clerical error or goodwill, that teacher had been placed at the
  correct step, establishing a precedent the board could not undo. The board member who most
  forcefully opposed Kint later had two sons in his junior high classroom. Kint treated them well.
