  - date: "1968-12-25"
    phase: in-country
    type: service
    tags:
      - { type: s, label: "POW Negotiation" }
    headline: "Operation CHICAGO FIRE, first meeting — December 25, 1968"
    body: >
      LTC Gibney led a five-man team, selected by Gen. Walter T. Kerwin (CG, III Corps),
      to a dry paddy about 50 miles northwest of Saigon (near the Cambodian border, in
      Tay Ninh Province), under a local cease-fire, after the NLF announced over
      Liberation Radio (18 Dec.) that it wished to release three American POWs. Gibney's
      team flew in by helicopter and landed on spotting a large NLF banner. Talks lasted
      more than two hours and ended in a procedural stalemate: per the New York Times
      (31 Dec. 1968), Gibney refused the NLF leader's protocol overtures — a seat, a
      drink of tea, a handshake — declining to grant any implied diplomatic recognition
      to the Front. Gibney himself proposed a second meeting when the talks appeared to
      be failing.

  - date: "1969-01-01"
    phase: in-country
    type: service
    tags:
      - { type: s, label: "POW Release" }
    headline: "Operation CHICAGO FIRE, second meeting — POWs released, January 1, 1969"
    body: >
      One week after the first meeting stalled, the NLF accepted Gibney's proposal for a
      second meeting, held at the same dry paddy. Gibney led the same five-man delegation
      (with Lt. Joseph V. Tourtelot, a Signal Corps photographer, replacing Capt. Ronald
      R. Tesch) and, with deputy/senior interpreter Maj. Jean A. Sauvageot, negotiated the
      release of three U.S. POWs from Viet Cong custody: Specialist 4 Thomas N. Jones
      (Lynnville, IN), Specialist 4 James W. Brigham (Ocala, FL), and Pfc. Donald G. Smith
      (Akron, PA) — all 21 years old, held more than a year. Per a UPI wire account (the
      Baltimore Afro-American, 4 Jan. 1969), the release occurred at 10:42 a.m.; a Viet
      Cong officer read the men's names as five Communist and five American
      representatives formally exchanged custody under an NLF banner, and Gibney himself
      called "Dustoff" — the signal for the pickup helicopter to land — once the exchange
      was complete. That UPI story gives Gibney's age as 38 and his hometown as Oakdale,
      N.Y., conflicting with the Clearwater, Fla. hometown in the NYT and Washington Star
      accounts — an unresolved discrepancy, not adjudicated here. About 24 hours earlier,
      Maj. James N. Rowe, a Special Forces officer held by the Vietcong for more than five
      years, had separately been recovered by government troops in the Mekong Delta — a
      simultaneous but unrelated event. See the New York Times (1 Jan. 1969), the
      Washington Star (2 Jan. 1969, preserved as a declassified CIA document), and the
      Baltimore Afro-American (4 Jan. 1969) for contemporary accounts.

  IDENTITY: John Vandever Gibney ("Jack"); born May 3, 1930; died April 2, 2022, age 91,
  after a short illness. Father Jesse Gibney was also a career Army officer. Brother:
  Jesse Lewis Gibney. Hometown at time of Vietnam service: Clearwater, Florida per the
  Washington Star (2 Jan 1969) and NYT (1 Jan 1969, "of Clearwater, Fla."), BUT the
  Baltimore Afro-American/UPI (4 Jan 1969) instead gives "of Oakdale, N.Y." — a direct
  conflict between contemporary wire sources, unresolved. The Afro-American piece does
  independently corroborate his age (38 in Jan 1969), consistent with the May 1930 birth
  year. Later lived in Northern Virginia (post-retirement), then Bainbridge Island, WA
  (from 1991). Wife: Barbara Apostolou (formerly Bucy), married 1974, died 2019 (44 years
  of marriage). Survived by stepsons Scott and Jeff Apostolou, granddaughter Kate
  Apostolou, nieces Karen Cummings and Ann McCaull.
  EDUCATION: USMA West Point, Class of 1952.
  SERVICE: 30 years in the U.S. Army; seven years in Vietnam (multiple tours); retired
  Colonel, 1982.
  COMMAND: Succeeded LTC Howard Petty as CO 2/8 Cav on 16 March 1968 (Eager Arms
  confirms: "LTC John V. Gibney assumed command of our battalion on 16 March"). RELIEVED
  BY COL FRANK LEONIDAS HENRY, ESTIMATED OCTOBER 1968 (2026-07-17, per Michael): Henry —
  independently confirmed by the Vietnam Helicopter Pilots Association and the Army
  Aviation Hall of Fame (Quad-A) as CO, 2nd Battalion, 8th Cavalry, in 1968-69, and almost
  certainly the "Colonel Henry" / "Stone Mountain 6" named in CPT Henry Colavita's memoir
  account of the 4 December 1968 "Chicken Valley" ambush — is now placed in the command
  chain directly after Gibney (see soldiers/henry-frank). The October 1968 start date is an
  ESTIMATE, not a confirmed date: Colavita's memoir describes roughly 6-month company/
  battalion command rotations as the general pattern in this unit, and 6 months from
  Gibney's 16 March 1968 start lands almost exactly on October. Michael's working theory is
  that Gibney rotated off battalion command to a 2nd Brigade-level staff position around
  this time — a battalion commander personally leading POW negotiations (below) was always
  an unusual assignment for someone still running a field unit — which would explain both
  the handoff to Henry and the POW-negotiation role. Plausible and the leading theory, but
  the exact handoff date is not yet confirmed by a primary source (unit command roster,
  ORLL, or similar); none of the three contemporary press accounts saved here (the two NYT
  pieces, the Baltimore Afro-American/UPI piece) give Gibney a unit or staff title at the
  time of the negotiation, only his rank and role leading the delegation. UPDATE
  (2026-07-17): a profile folder Michael supplied for Henry independently pins the end of
  Henry's 2/8 Cav command to approximately March 1969 (a 1969 hometown-newspaper clipping
  and his 1977 obituary — see soldiers/henry-frank) — a ~6-month tour from an October 1968
  start fits that end date cleanly, circumstantial support for the October estimate without
  yet confirming the exact handoff day. See events/operation-sheridan-sabre-1968-12-04 and
  documents/colavita-henry/colavita-henry-verbal-account-dec4-1968.
  By December 1968, Gibney was leading the American delegation
