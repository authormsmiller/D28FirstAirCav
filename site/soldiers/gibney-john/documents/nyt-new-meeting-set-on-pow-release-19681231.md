SAIGON, South Vietnam, Dec. 30 — The United States military command
announced tonight that it would meet with representatives of the National
Liberation Front Wednesday to discuss for the second time the release of
three American prisoners of war.

In a statement broadcast on the Armed Forces Radio station in Saigon, the
United States said that since the allies had decided against a New Year's
cease-fire, a special corridor "free of military action" would be
established for the meeting. The corridor, a little more than a mile wide and
six miles long, will lead northeast from the Cambodian border to the same dry
paddy where the five-man delegations of the United States and the National
Liberation Front, or Vietcong, met on Christmas, 51 miles northwest of Saigon.

The Armed Forces Radio announcer said that the American statement was being
made with the concurrence of the South Vietnamese Government. **The leader of
the United States delegation, Lieut. Col. John V. Gibney, suggested a second
meeting when the initial talks appeared to be failing** to gain the freedom of
the American prisoners. The leader of the delegation from the Front indicated
a willingness to talk again, but did not formally accept the offer until two
days ago in a broadcast on their clandestine Liberation Radio.

### Vietcong Broadcast Cited

In the broadcast this evening, the United States called attention to an
announcement on Liberation Radio last Thursday that said the three prisoners
had been released. In view of this broadcast, the United States said, it
"sees no reason why the three prisoners cannot be produced at the meeting on
Jan. 1."

The men have been identified as Specialist 4 James W. Brigham of Ocala, Fla.,
Specialist 4 Thomas N. Jones of Lynnville, Ind., and Pfc. Donald G. Smith of
Akron, Pa.

All United States and South Vietnamese statements to the Liberation Front
about the three prisoners have been made through Lieut. Gen. Walter Kerwin,
who commands the region that includes the meeting place. This is an apparent
attempt to deal with the enemy at the lowest possible acceptable level. The
allies are intent upon recovering the three prisoners without according the
Front formal diplomatic recognition.

The United States said that it was prepared to meet with "opposing forces" to
discuss the time and place of prisoner releases, "together with such local
arrangements as are required to ensure that the releases take place in
peaceful and appropriate circumstances." "We see no necessity," the American
statement added, "to discuss any other matters since the objective is to
arrange for the release of the prisoners."

The Vietcong charged in their broadcast two days ago that the first talks had
failed because the American delegates had "refused to discuss the necessary
preparatory procedures for releasing and receiving" the three men. According
to well-informed Americans, no progress was made because the leader of the
United States delegation refused to acknowledge the Vietcong leader's
attempts to establish protocol precedents. **The American leader, the sources
said, refused to accept either a seat or a drink of tea or to shake hands
with the Vietcong leader.**

The time set by the Vietcong for Wednesday's meeting is 9 A.M. (8 P.M.
Eastern standard time Tuesday). According to the United States broadcast, the
corridor will be established at 6 A.M. and maintained until three hours after
the meeting has ended. As in the initial meeting, the allies intend to keep
all armed troops slightly more than a half mile away from the meeting place.

### Incidents During Truce

Meanwhile, allied military spokesmen said that in the first 12 hours of the
72-hour New Year cease-fire unilaterally ordered by the Vietcong, half a
dozen enemy-initiated actions had been reported. Most of these were mortar
bombardments that caused little damage and few casualties.

Premier Tran Van Huong announced two days ago that the South Vietnamese would
not observe a New Year's cease-fire because the enemy had violated the mutual
Christmas truce more than 100 times.

In war action during the last 24 hours, there were two small engagements
within 20 miles of Saigon. Several major ammunition caches were discovered.
In the first engagement, about 12 miles north of the city in Giadinh
Province, a Government paratrooper and an enemy guard were killed; the enemy
soldiers had been watching a cache of 15,000 rounds of rifle ammunition and
about 80 mortar rounds. In the second engagement, five enemy soldiers were
killed when their platoon clashed with a company of Government militia about
18 miles southwest of Saigon in Longan Province. There were no Government
losses reported.

Sixteen miles west of Saigon, on the Vamcondong River, United States Navy
patrols found 3,000 rounds of rifle ammunition, 8 mortar rounds, 12 mines and
2 rocket-grenade boosters. The cache was the twelfth uncovered along the
waterway since Dec. 6. Some military authorities said that the increasing
number of such discoveries, along with the rising number of engagements close
to Saigon, indicate that the enemy may be planning another thrust against the
city.
