## Overview

Camp Radcliff was the **1st Cavalry Division's base camp** in Vietnam — the place D Company came
home to between operations for the whole Central Highlands era. It sat on **Highway QL-19 at An
Khê**, roughly 60 km inland from Qui Nhơn and 60 km short of Pleiku, in **Bình Định Province**.
Built beginning **27 August 1965** by the 70th Engineer Battalion, it grew into the **largest
helicopter base in the world** at the time, laid out to handle the division's 400-plus aircraft.

For the men who had just crossed the Pacific on the **USNS Geiger** and ridden trucks and Chinooks
up from the coast, Radcliff was the first ground they held in Vietnam. D Company flew in around **20
September 1965** and was based here until the division moved north to Camp Evans in **January
1968**.

## Namesake

The camp was named for **Major Donald G. Radcliff** (1928–1965), executive officer of the 1st
Squadron, 9th Cavalry and the **1st Cavalry Division's first combat death**. In August 1965 Radcliff
was part of the **site-selection team** — under Brig. Gen. John M. Wright — that scoured Bình Định
Province for the division's base camp **while the division itself was still at sea, crossing the
Pacific**. He helped choose the very ground the base would stand on.

With the team in country and the 7th Marines about to launch **Operation Starlite**, Radcliff
volunteered to fly support. At dawn on **18 August 1965**, piloting a UH-1B gunship escorting the
airmobile assault into **LZ Blue** — which was ringed by the 60th VC Battalion — near Chu Lai, he
pinpointed the enemy and laid down suppressive fire so the troop-carriers could land, and was
mortally wounded at the controls. He was **37 years old**, on his first day of combat. Radcliff was
posthumously awarded the **Distinguished Flying Cross** (USARV General Orders 372, 1 Sep 1965); the
base he had helped select was formally dedicated in his name on **21 February 1966**. His name is on
the Wall at panel 2E, line 59.

## Location

The base lay at approximately **13.993° N, 108.6485° E**, astride QL-19 in the An Khê District of
Bình Định Province, at about 1,380 feet of elevation. An **An Khê Airfield** to the southeast could
land C-130 transports. The coordinates are confirmed against the base record; the site is today used
by the People's Army of Vietnam.

## The Golf Course

The base's nickname came from how it was built. Brig. Gen. Wright wanted the underlying grass kept
but cut close to the ground — to hold down the dust helicopter rotors would otherwise throw up — and
called the cleared, tree-stripped expanse a **"golf course."** The name stuck. An advance party of
roughly **1,040 soldiers**, officers and privates alike **swinging grub hooks**, cleared the
six-square-kilometer area **by hand**, while a brigade of the **101st Airborne** secured the
perimeter around them as they worked. The division painted its **shoulder insignia on nearby Hòn
Cong Mountain**, a landmark visible for miles, and ringed the camp with a **26-kilometer perimeter,
the Green Line**, watched from three-man towers spaced every 50 meters.

The base was not immune to attack. On the night of **3 September 1966**, the Viet Cong put **119
mortar rounds** onto the Golf Course in about five minutes, killing four soldiers, wounding 76, and
damaging 77 helicopters. Further mortar and sapper attacks struck in October and November 1968 and
again in November 1969, under the units that followed the 1st Cav.

## Tenure

The **1st Cavalry Division** held Camp Radcliff as its base camp from September 1965 until it moved
to Camp Evans in **January 1968**. The **173rd Airborne Brigade** then occupied it into mid-1969,
followed by the **4th Infantry Division** through December 1970. For D Company, 2/8 Cav, Radcliff
was the constant in the back of the war's first two-plus years — the place the company returned to
from the Pleiku Campaign and the Bình Định operations that followed.

The American chapter closed when the 4th Infantry Division left in December 1970 and the base passed
to the South Vietnamese Army. The site is used today by the People's Army of Vietnam.

## The fall of An Khê, 1975

Camp Radcliff's end came in **March 1975**, a month before the fall of Saigon. As the North
Vietnamese drove into the Central Highlands, the South Vietnamese garrison withdrew and An Khê — the
helicopter base the 1st Cav had carved out of the brush nine years earlier — **fell without a
fight**.

For the Americans who had served there, the news landed hard. John Samuel Tieman, who had been at
Camp Radcliff with the 4th Infantry Division in 1970, watched An Khê turn red on the evening-news
map: *"Camp Radcliff fell without a fight. The South Vietnamese Army just ran… And I wept
bitterly."* What he grieved was one name and all of them at once — **Specialist 4 Eric Williams**,
killed years before Tieman's tour while building the bridge at An Khê that carried his name, whose
plaque Tieman read every time he passed. *"Because,"* he wrote, *"it was all in vain."*

That grief — private, isolating, fixed on a single name out of all the names — was how a great many
Vietnam veterans met 1975. Williams' bridge stands on this page as a marker of it.

If you or someone in your family was at Camp Radcliff — the Golf Course, the Green Line towers, the
insignia on Hòn Cong Mountain, or the 1966 mortar attack — we'd welcome a name, a photo, or a
memory to add to the record.
