## September 6–26, 1967 — PFC Michael Paul Santoroski

> **Incident page.** Primary source: newspaper obituary published in the Hazleton, Pennsylvania
> area, October 1967 (Santoroski family copy), supplemented by Virtual Wall and VVMF records.
> Santoroski's position on September 6 and the name of the Philippine hospital where he died
> are not established in available sources.

On **September 6, 1967**, **PFC Michael Paul Santoroski** stepped on a land mine somewhere in
**Binh Dinh Province** and was gravely wounded. He was twenty years old, a mortar crewman
with D Company, 2nd Battalion, 8th Cavalry, and he had been in Vietnam for exactly one month.
Twenty days later, on **September 26**, he died of his wounds at a U.S. Army hospital in the
**Philippines**. He is inscribed on the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, **Panel 27E, Line 16**.

### Seven weeks into his tour

Santoroski arrived in-country on **August 6, 1967**, assigned to D Company as an 11C10 —
an indirect fire infantryman, mortar crew. The company was operating in **Binh Dinh Province**
under the umbrella of **Operation Pershing**, the 1st Cavalry Division's year-long mission to
destroy the NVA and Viet Cong presence on the **Bong Son Plain**. Land mines and booby traps
were among the operation's most constant hazards: the plain's paddies, dikes, and paths were
threaded with pressure-activated and command-detonated devices, planted by both the 95th NVA
Regiment and local VC units. Seven weeks into his tour, Santoroski found one.

The mine took **both legs below the knees**. He was evacuated from the field — first to Vietnam,
then to a U.S. Army hospital in the **Philippines**.

### The letter

Four days after the wounding, he wrote home:

> *"Mom, please have courage. It's hard for me and you can make it easier if you are strong.
> God had planned for me. I accept it for I have my whole life ahead of me and I'm determined
> not to let this get me down."*

He told his family he expected to be transferred first to **Tokyo**, then to **Walter Reed Army
Hospital** in Washington — the standard evacuation chain for seriously wounded soldiers going
home. He was resolved. He was going to get through this.

He was twenty years old and had just lost both legs below the knees. He died sixteen days after
writing that letter.

### Died of wounds — twenty days later

PFC Santoroski died at the Philippines Army hospital on **September 26, 1967**, twenty days
after the wounding. The VVMF lists his casualty province as **"Unknown"** — the standard
designation when a soldier dies outside Vietnam, with no Vietnamese province on record. He was
brought home and buried after a high mass of requiem at **St. Stanislaus Church** in Hazleton,
Pennsylvania — his grandmother's parish — and interred in the parish cemetery.

He was survived by his parents, **Joseph F. and Marie E. (Lickvar) Santoroski**, of Kingston,
New York; his brothers **Robert, Stephen,** and **Thomas**; and his sister **Barbara**.

### A Pershing casualty — not a Bolling casualty

One detail matters for the unit record: Santoroski's **official casualty date is September 26**,
which falls inside the window of **Operation Bolling** (September 19 – October 14, 1967), when
2/8 Cav was temporarily attached to the 173rd Airborne Brigade west of Tuy Hoa. He had nothing
to do with Bolling. He was wounded on **September 6**, more than two weeks before Bolling began,
in the **Pershing AO in Binh Dinh Province**. He was already in a Philippine hospital when the
battalion moved south. His death is a Pershing casualty, dated to the window of Bolling only
because DOW cases carry the date of death rather than the date of wounding.
