## November 1969 – April 1970

> **Operations context page.** Primary source for the February–April 1970 period: 1st Cavalry
> Division Operational Report – Lessons Learned, period ending 30 April 1970 (DTIC AD0512505).
> The November 1969 – January 1970 period is documented at the division level pending review of
> the corresponding ORLL. No formal operation name was designated for this period.

When **Operation Toan Thang III** ended on October 31, 1969, the 1st Brigade of the 1st Cavalry
Division did not change course. The same battalions — the **2nd Battalions of the 5th, 7th, and
8th Cavalry**, with elements of the **11th Armored Cavalry Regiment** — continued working the
same terrain: **western War Zone C**, the jungle corridor along the Cambodian border in Tay Ninh
Province, threaded with NVA supply routes, base areas, and rice caches feeding the enemy's III
Corps operations. Toan Thang III had a name and formal dates; what followed had neither. The
mission was the same.

For six months — November 1969 through April 1970 — the 1st Brigade conducted what the division's
own operational report describes only as ongoing efforts "to interdict enemy lines of communication
and supply and to destroy enemy forces, installations, and equipment." On May 1, 1970, that same
border corridor became the launch point for the **Cambodian Incursion**.

### The interdiction mission

War Zone C in late 1969 and early 1970 was a high-volume logistical corridor. The NVA's
**9th VC Division** — principally its **272nd** and **95th NVA Regiments** — used the dense
jungle and established trail networks to move rice, ammunition, and personnel south toward
the population centers of III Corps. The 1st Brigade's job was to cut those routes.

The tools were consistent across the period: air assault into suspected cache areas, automatic
ambushes on known trails, artillery on observed movement, and ground sweeps to find and destroy
what had been pre-positioned. The 1st Cav Div ORLL for this period records the effectiveness
of the automatic ambush program specifically — 254 enemy killed, 7 captured, 127 weapons
recovered across February through April 1970 alone, with no U.S. casualties from enemy
counter-ambush actions. Rice was the other primary target: in northern Tay Ninh Province, a
single cache found by C Company, 2/8 Cav on March 1, 1970 at grid XT185995 yielded **133.5
tons** — two months' supply for all NVA units operating in the area.

### Fire support bases — mobile by design

The 1st Cavalry Division operated its fire support bases in this corridor as deliberate
temporary platforms, not fixed installations. The division's ORLL states it directly: **"light
fire support bases were moved frequently, thus limiting the enemy's opportunity for
reconnaissance."** FSB Carol, FSB Tina, FSB Flasher, FSB Jay — all active in the 2/8 Cav
area of operations during this period — were positioned and repositioned on airmobile timelines.
**FB Mary Gwen** was opened by 2/8 Cav on February 24, 1970 as the battalion's firebase in the
border area. None of these were permanent.

This mobility served a tactical purpose — it complicated enemy targeting. But it also means
that establishing the precise position of any company on any given day requires the battalion's
own daily staff journal, which is held at the National Archives (NARA RG 472) and has not been
reviewed for this period.

### February 1970 — the heaviest month

February was the costliest month for D Company in this period. The battalion entered the month
already in sustained contact with elements of the **95th NVA Regiment**, which was identified
in fighting near **FSB Tina on February 4** — D Company and at least one other company, sweeping
south of the firebase, caught an estimated company-sized NVA force and fought a sharp engagement
that cost 4 US KIA and 9 WIA; 44 NVA were killed and 25 AK-47s captured.

Ten days later, on **February 14**, C Company, 2/8 Cav, reinforced by a troop of the 1st
Squadron, 11th Armored Cavalry, made contact with elements of the **272nd NVA Regiment** at
grid XT251704. The fight lasted hours. When it ended, **10 Americans were dead and 30 wounded**;
46 NVA were killed, a T-54 tank was destroyed, and documents confirmed the unit as the 272nd.
It was the battalion's hardest single-day fight of the period.

Four days after that action — on **February 18** — **PFC Henry Tony Rava** of D Company died of
wounds from friendly artillery, rocket, or mortar fire. He was twenty years old and had been in
Vietnam four months and eleven days.

By month's end, D Company had also lost **PFC Kenneth Frierson** (January 25) and
**SP4 Fred Flashner** (February 28). **SP4 Gordon Ware** (March 6), **SP4 David Brockmeier**
(March 7), and **SP4 Robert Jackson** (March 17) followed in March. Seven D Company soldiers
died in the War Zone C corridor in roughly ten weeks — the company's highest-density loss
cluster of the war's middle years.

### March–April — mounting pressure

The significant contacts logged in the ORLL for March show the same pattern of sustained enemy
presence in the XT grid: A Company, C Company, and D Company of 2/8 Cav each appear in
separate engagements, fighting NVA units in and around bunker complexes along the XT17/18 and
XT04/07 grid corridors. On **March 8**, C Company's extraction from its position was turned into
a running fight — the aircraft lifting the company out drew heavy AK-47 and .30-caliber fire;
one helicopter was destroyed, two crewmen wounded. The company was pulled to **FSB Carolyn**.

Enemy activity was not only ground-level. The 1st Brigade's fire support bases were coming
under increasingly heavy standoff attack. On **March 9**, FSB Flasher received approximately
175 rounds of 82mm mortar and 107mm rocket fire. FSB Jay — occupied by 2-7 Cav beginning
March 18 — was struck by elements of the **271st NVA Regiment** in a coordinated assault on
the night of **March 29–30**, one of the most intense attacks on a 1st Cav firebase during the
war.

### FSB Illingworth — March 29–30, 1970

In the early morning hours of March 29, **FSB Jay** (grid XT039750) — garrisoned by elements of
the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry and two artillery batteries — was attacked by the **271st NVA
Regiment** supported by a heavy indirect fire preparation. The attack breached the wire. Enemy
soldiers entered the perimeter and reached the howitzers before direct artillery fire at close
range repulsed them. The fight lasted hours; when it ended, **13 Americans were dead and 53
wounded**, two 105mm howitzers were damaged, and 74 NVA lay dead inside and outside the wire.

The 2nd Battalion, 8th Cavalry was at **FSB Illingworth** during this action — close enough
that the 2/8 Cav artillery liaison officer at Illingsworth observed the firing at Jay and
relayed communications for the 2-7 Cav's fire coordination net when the infantry TOC lost
its radios. Illingsworth itself, in the same AO, would face its own large-scale assault on
**April 1, 1970** — an attack in which C and E Companies of 2/8 Cav were engaged and SGT
Peter Lemon of E Company would earn the Medal of Honor. That action falls outside D Company's
direct experience in this period but within the battalion's, and marks the operational high-
water mark of the enemy's effort to strike American firebases before the cross-border assault
reversed the pressure.

### April — the threshold

Through April 1970, the 1st Brigade continued operations in the northern and western portions
of the War Zone C AO as enemy activity remained elevated. On **May 1, 1970**, the division
launched the **Cambodian Incursion** — crossing the border that had defined the edge of
American ground operations for years. The War Zone C corridor that 2/8 Cav and the 1st Brigade
had worked since late 1968 became the staging area for the assault. The interdiction they had
maintained through six months without a named operation name was the foundation for what
came next.
