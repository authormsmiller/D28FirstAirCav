## February 18, 1970 — PFC Henry Tony Rava

> **Incident page.** Primary sources: Virtual Wall, VVMF, and Honor States casualty records;
> 1st Cavalry Division Operational Report – Lessons Learned for the period ending 30 April 1970
> (DTIC AD0512505). The specific circumstances of the friendly fire incident — which unit's
> artillery or mortar fire struck him, and the exact position of D Company on February 18 —
> are not established in available sources.

On **February 18, 1970**, **PFC Henry Tony Rava** died of wounds in **Tay Ninh Province**. He
was twenty years old, a rifleman with D Company, 2nd Battalion, 8th Cavalry, and he had been
in Vietnam since October. The fire that killed him was friendly — artillery, rocket, or mortar
from U.S. or allied forces. He is inscribed on the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, **Panel 13W,
Line 31**.

### The casualty codes

The Army's casualty record for Rava carries a combination that appears infrequently on the Wall:
**Hostile / Died of Wounds / Misadventure / Artillery-Rocket-Mortar / Ground Casualty**. Each
element is precise. "Hostile" reflects the combat environment — he was in a war zone, under
the conditions of war. "Died of Wounds" means he survived initial wounding but died before
medical intervention could save him. "Misadventure" is the Army's term for death caused by
friendly fire rather than the enemy. "Artillery, Rocket, or Mortar" identifies the weapon type.
Together the codes record that PFC Rava was struck by friendly artillery, rocket, or mortar
fire and died of those wounds on February 18, 1970.

The Purple Heart followed automatically — the hostile classification made him eligible. A soldier
killed by friendly fire in a combat zone receives the same recognition as a soldier killed by
the enemy, because the war put him there.

### Where the battalion was

In February 1970, the 2nd Battalion, 8th Cavalry was operating deep in **western War Zone C**,
a corridor of jungle, trail networks, and Cambodian border terrain in Tay Ninh Province. The
battalion was under the **1st Brigade**, whose mission — as the 1st Cavalry Division's own
operational report states — was "to interdict enemy lines of communication and supply and to
destroy enemy forces, installations, and equipment." There was no single named operation.
The 1st Brigade, with the 2-8 Cav, 2-7 Cav, 2-5 Cav, and elements of the 11th Armored
Cavalry Regiment, was conducting continuous operations to disrupt NVA supply movements through
War Zone C toward the population centers to the south. This was the buildup period preceding
the **Cambodian Incursion** of May 1970 — the division was pressing hard against the border
corridor that fed the NVA's III Corps units.

### Four days after the heaviest contact of the month

The four days before Rava's death were the bloodiest the battalion had seen in weeks. On
**February 4**, elements operating near **FSB Tina** clashed with an estimated company-sized
NVA force, losing 4 US KIA and 9 WIA; captured documents identified the **C-21 Reconnaissance
Company, 95th NVA Regiment**. On **February 14**, C Company, 2/8 Cav — reinforced by a troop
of the 1-11 Armored Cavalry Regiment — made contact with elements of the **272nd NVA Regiment**
at grid XT251704. That fight lasted hours and cost **10 US KIA and 30 US WIA**; 46 NVA were
killed. The 272nd Regiment was one of the 9th VC Division's main-force units, well-equipped
and fighting hard in the border corridor.

PFC Rava died four days after that action, on February 18. The ORLL records no significant
enemy contact on that date for the battalion — which is consistent with the nature of friendly
fire deaths. They do not arise from engagements; they arise from the constant artillery and
mortar fires that surrounded ground operations in a high-intensity AO, and from the inevitable
human and mechanical failures of those fires. A fire mission goes long. A short round falls
inside the perimeter. A call for fire is plotted incorrectly. The battalion was surrounded by
friendly artillery in February 1970 — FSB Tina, FSB Flasher, FSB Carolyn, FSB Jay, and others
were all active in the 1st Brigade's AO during this period, collectively maintaining continuous
fires to suppress enemy movement. Rava was killed by one of those fires.

### Why the position is not established

The specific fire support base or patrol position D Company occupied on February 18 is not
recorded in available sources. This is not simply a gap in the documentary record — it is partly
a consequence of how the division operated.

The 1st Cavalry Division's Operational Report for this period states directly that "light fire
support bases were moved frequently, thus limiting the enemy's opportunity for reconnaissance."
In western War Zone C in early 1970, FSBs were mobile by design. FSB Carolyn, FSB Tina, FSB
Flasher, FSB Jay — none of them were fixed positions that a battalion occupied for months.
They were temporary platforms, moved on airmobile timelines, positioned and repositioned to
maintain fire coverage as ground units maneuvered. D Company could have been at a temporary
FSB, in a night defensive position in the jungle, or on patrol. Without the 2/8 Cav daily
staff journal (NARA RG 472) for February 18, 1970, the position cannot be confirmed.

### Henry Rava — from Mt Angel, Oregon

Henry Tony Rava was from **Mt Angel**, a small farming community in Marion County, Oregon,
in the Willamette Valley. He was born in 1949, drafted from Oregon, and arrived in Vietnam on
**October 7, 1969** — assigned to D Company as a light-weapons infantryman (MOS 11B10). He
was twenty years old and had been in-country **four months and eleven days** when he died.
He was brought home to Mt Angel and buried at **Calvary Cemetery**.

He is one of eleven D Company soldiers who died in 1970. Three others died in the weeks
immediately around him: **SP4 Kevin Frierson** (January 25), **SP4 Fred Flashner** (February
28), and **SP4 Gordon Ware** (March 6) — all in the same border corridor, all in the months
before the Cambodian Incursion that the division was preparing.

### A classmate's account

In January 2023, **Charles Simpson** — a Military Policeman with the **1st Infantry Division**
at **Lai Khe Base Camp** from 1969 to 1970 — left an account on a Vietnam War travel website.
He had gone to high school with Henry Rava. "I had a friend killed there Henry T. Rava, who
I had went to High School with that died of shrapnel wounds," he wrote.

The identification is solid — Simpson named him by full name and personal relationship. The
description of **shrapnel wounds** aligns with the Army casualty codes (artillery, rocket, or
mortar) and is the kind of physical detail a friend carries accurately across fifty years.

Simpson also connected Rava's death to an ammo dump explosion at the Lai Khe airstrip, and
placed the death "back in Seattle" — but neither detail holds up. Lai Khe was a 1st Infantry
Division installation; Rava served with the 1st Cavalry Division in Tay Ninh Province, over
fifty miles away. Rava is buried at Calvary Cemetery in Mt Angel, Oregon, not Seattle. The
ammo dump explosion at Lai Khe was real — another commenter, Otis Felton, a postal sergeant
stationed there in the same period, described being knocked unconscious by it — but it belongs
to Simpson's own experience at his own post. The most likely reading is that Simpson heard of
a classmate's death and, at some point in the fifty years since, the most vivid event from his
own tour became attached to it in memory. His hedge — "I do believe I remember" — is honest.

What the account establishes: **Henry Rava had a friend from home in Vietnam.** Someone who
knew him before the war was serving in-country at the same time.
