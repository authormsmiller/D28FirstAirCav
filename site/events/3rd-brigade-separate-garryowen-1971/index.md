## 3rd Brigade (Separate) — "Garryowen Task Force," 1971

> **Context page — brigade level.** Drawn from the Senior Officer Debriefing Report of **BG
> Jonathan R. Burton**, commanding general of the 3rd Brigade (Separate), 1st Cavalry Division,
> period **10 April – 13 December 1971** (DTIC AD0520447). It frames how the brigade operated;
> the full report is linked below to read in full. Treat the connection to specific D Company
> actions as **highly likely context, not established fact.**

### The division standdown — 26 March 1971

The 1st Cavalry Division formally stood down in Vietnam on **26 March 1971**, in a brief ceremony at the division's rear headquarters area at **Bien Hoa** (moved from the long-planned Phuoc Vinh site only that morning). Present were **Gen. Creighton Abrams** (commander of U.S. forces in Vietnam), **Lt. Gen. Michael Davison** (II Field Force), **Lt. Gen. Nguyen Van Minh** (III Military Region), and division commander **MG George W. Putnam Jr.** The posting of the division colors closed the ceremony; those colors were shipped to **Fort Hood, Texas,** to be reflagged for the **1st Cavalry Division (TRICAP)**, which assumed the assets of the 1st Armored Division. The division had arrived in Vietnam in **September 1965.** With the division gone, its 3rd Brigade remained behind as the separate "Garryowen Task Force" described below.

*Source: "Standdown ceremony typifies First Team," [CAVALAIR, 14 April 1971](/documents/unit/cavalair-19710414/) — the 1st Air Cav Division weekly's standdown special edition (attached to this event under Documents).*

**The brigade.** When the 1st Cavalry Division stood down and redeployed in the spring of 1971,
its 3rd Brigade remained in Vietnam as a **separate brigade — the "Garryowen Task Force,"**
headquartered at **Bien Hoa** — to continue the division's mission. **2/8 Cav (D Company's
parent battalion) was one of its four maneuver battalions** (with the 2/5, 1/7, and 1/12 Cav,
plus F Troop, 9th Cav and D Company, 229th Assault Helicopter Battalion). BG Jonathan R. Burton
assumed command on 13 April 1971.

**Mission.** To conduct operations against enemy main and local force elements, their lines of
communication, and base areas within the AO; to support the combined campaign and the Community
Defense and Local Development plans in Long Khanh and Binh Tuy provinces; and to be prepared to
execute contingency plans.

**Area of operations.** About **3,500 square miles** — all of Binh Tuy, most of **Long Khanh**,
and parts of Bien Hoa and Lam Dong — roughly **two-thirds of the old division's AO held with
one-third of its assets.** Terrain ranged from coastal plain and single-canopy jungle south of
QL-1 to the double- and triple-canopy **War Zone D** of Long Khanh north and west of QL-20. Key
enemy: the **MR-7 Rear Service Group** (operating from Nui Be), Base Areas 305 and 306, and the
**33rd NVA Regiment**, on which brigade operations were oriented. Each of the four battalions was
assigned a slice of the AO, its boundaries shifted "as intelligence and operations indicated"
the enemy had moved.

**How the brigade fought — and why it matters to the April 1971 D Company events.** Burton
describes a force in active contact **less than fifteen percent of the time,** oriented heavily
on reconnaissance to "find and fix" an enemy who avoided contact, then reacting explosively. The
signature method: a Ranger team or Air Cav element locates an enemy position or bunker complex;
a **"Pink Team"** (an armed light observation helicopter and a Cobra) develops the target; an
aero-rifle **"Blue Team"** goes in; and a one-company **Quick Reaction Force**, held on 30-minute
notice, combat-assaults into the target area. Burton specifically describes **"Mini-Cav
operations — short-duration, platoon-sized, airmobile operations executed on the basis of
reliable but highly perishable intelligence."** That is, in the brigade commander's own words,
the kind of operation that produced the **April 20, 1971 contact near FSB Fontaine**, when Range
Platoon air-assaulted into the Gia Ray area and struck a fortified enemy rear-service bunker
complex.

**Caveats — highly likely context, not proof.** This is a senior officer's retrospective
debriefing, not a day-by-day record; it frames the brigade's doctrine and AO but does not
narrate D Company's specific actions. The report's general AO enemy (the MR-7 Rear Service Group
/ 33rd NVA Regiment) and the "83rd NVA rear service unit" named in the April 20 event are the
same *type* of formation but different designations — to be reconciled against the 2/8 Cav daily
staff journal (NARA RG 472).

### Brigade life — the rotation, Vung Tau R&R, and morale programs

Burton's report also documents the rhythm of a Skytrooper's life in 1971 — the kind of detail
veterans often remember long after the operations blur together.

**The rotation (Personnel Management, para. 1).** Because of the vastness of the AO, rifle
companies operated largely independently, each within the artillery fan of a firebase. The
pattern: about **fifteen days in the "bush,"** resupplied by helicopter every three or four days
with ammunition, rations, and water — and, when a set-down LZ allowed, **a hot meal and ice
cream, a change of clothes, mail, newspapers, and other comfort items.** Then **five days on a
firebase** providing security, refitting, and undergoing bunker and marksmanship training. And
every **45 days, each rifle company and recon platoon rotated to the First Team R&R Center for a
three-day standdown** — the in-country center that **opened at Vung Tau in March 1971.** Burton
calls "Vung Tau R&R" a privilege "much sought after by all Skytroopers" and a major morale
factor: knowing you would be back at the firebase every fifteen days and get in-country R&R
every forty-five "provided a great lift for the individual rifleman."

**Morale programs (para. 2).** Two stand out: the **"Flying PX"** — PX services flown (and
trucked, as a "rolling PX") out to the firebases, with contact teams to help with financial,
personnel, and legal problems in the field — and **"King for a Day,"** in which each week one
company, battery, or aviation-platoon commander was brought to brigade headquarters for
recognition: VIP treatment, the General's briefings, a tour of the area of operations with the
Commanding General, and a 1st Cavalry Division memento.

Source: 3rd Brigade (Separate) Senior Officer Debriefing Report (AD0520447), Personnel
Management.
